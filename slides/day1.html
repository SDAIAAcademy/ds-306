The session is about what a corpus is before it is data: a sample of people who chose to write, through channels that shape what they write.

A corpus is a sample

Complaints sample the people who write, not the people who are affected

Who writes

Residents with the app, time and confidence to complain. A street with older residents may suffer more and write less.

How they write

The channel shapes the text: a call-centre transcript, a form field, a social post, an email pasted from a PDF.

What it can answer

"What do complaints say?" directly. "Where are services worst?" only with a check against something else, such as inspections.

What goes wrong. A rate of complaints is read as a rate of problems. It is a rate of people who wrote.

Ask the room for one group that writes less than it suffers. Answers usually include older residents, workers without the app, and people who gave up after an earlier report. Day 4 returns to this when the corrected rate is reported: correcting the labels does not correct who wrote.

What a computer sees

Text pasted from a PDF looks Arabic and is made of different characters

"تحية"  →  U+062A U+062D U+064A U+0629      # letters
"ﺕحﻱة"  →  U+FE95 U+062D U+FEF1 U+0629      # presentation forms

unicodedata.normalize("NFKC", "ﺕحﻱة") == "تحية"   # True
  • Presentation forms are the shapes a PDF stores; NFKC folds them back into letters
  • To a classifier, the two spellings are two unrelated words

From Lab 1.2: the first complaint with presentation forms, an email. Type both strings in a notebook and compare them with == to show they differ, then apply NFKC. Unicode normalisation forms are defined in Unicode Standard Annex 15. Sources: Python unicodedata documentation.

Every rule is a decision

A normalisation rule removes noise and, sometimes, a difference that mattered

  • ة to ه merges a feminine ending with a pronoun suffix
  • Digits merge ٢٣ and 23, which is right for counting streets
  • Marks remove emphasis the writer chose
  • 53 normalised tokens in this corpus came from both a final ة and a final ه

The habit

Keep the raw text in its own column. Write each rule with its layer name. Rerun with a layer off when a result surprises you.

What goes wrong. Normalised text overwrites the raw text, and a rule that turns out wrong cannot be undone.

The 53 comes from the stretch of Lab 1.2. Most are the same word spelled two ways, such as الحديقة and الحديقه, which is what the rule is for. Ask the room for a pair that is two words; in Gulf text مره ("very") and مرة ("once") are the classic example.

Not all copies are the same

A copy, a resubmission and a campaign need three different decisions

Exact copy

The same text twice within days. One citizen pressed send twice. Keep the first.

Resubmission

The same complaint with a sentence added: "this is my second report". One citizen, one complaint. Keep the first, note the repeat.

Campaign

Many residents paste one message and change a few words. Many complaints, one issue. Keep all, flag them.

The three units of session 1 come back: a copy changes the message count only; a campaign changes the issue count. The keep rule in Lab 1.3 encodes these three decisions.

Comparing texts

Shingles turn a text into a set of overlapping five-character pieces

المجالمجارمجاريجاري اري طري طاي طاف طافح
  • Normalise first, so spelling variants share shingles
  • Mask long numbers: two copies with different phone numbers should still match
  • Keep short numbers: Street 12 and Street 46 are different places

The chips are the first shingles of "المجاري طافحة". Characters rather than words suit Arabic: a prefix such as ال or a suffix does not stop two forms of one word from sharing most of their shingles. The masking rule is the first line of the lab's prepare() function.

One number for overlap

Jaccard similarity is the shared shingles over all the shingles of the two texts

|A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B|
1 for identical sets, 0 for nothing shared
  • Two campaign messages written in different words: 0.21
  • Two unrelated complaints: 0.03
  • A resubmission and its original: usually above 0.7
Same issue, different words. Jaccard finds copied wording, not the same problem described twice. The campaign is found as several groups, one per template.

Numbers from Lab 1.3, task 1. The 0.21 is the honest surprise: two residents complaining about the same sewer in their own words are not near-duplicates by text. That is correct behaviour for this tool, and the reason a campaign is found as a few groups that the keep rule then flags by size.

Fast candidates

MinHash compares 20,000 complaints without comparing every pair

Signature64 minimum hash values per complaint
→
AgreementThe share of equal positions estimates Jaccard
→
Bands16 bands of 4: equal in one band makes a candidate
→
ConfirmThe true Jaccard on candidates only

All pairs would be about 200 million comparisons. The bands leave about 103,000 candidates.

The probability that two signatures agree at one position equals the Jaccard similarity of the sets; that is the whole trick. Bands tune the trade-off: more rows per band means fewer, closer candidates. Sources: Leskovec, Rajaraman and Ullman, Mining of Massive Datasets, chapter 3 (Finding Similar Items). The lab writes it in numpy to see it.

Lab 1.4, step 2

One pattern covers both digit systems, the country code and the separators

D = "[0-9٠-٩]"
PHONE = rf"(?:\+?(?:966|٩٦٦)[\s\-]?|[0٠])?[5٥]{D}(?:[\s\-]?{D}){{7}}(?!{D})"
ID    = rf"(?<!{D})[12١٢]{D}{{9}}(?!{D})"
EMAIL = r"[\w.+-]+@[\w-]+(?:\.[\w-]+)+"
  • The digit class keeps the citizen's text as written: no translation before masking
  • Phones first, then emails, then identity numbers

Walk the phone pattern left to right: optional country code or leading zero, a 5, then eight more digits with optional separators, and nothing digit-like after. Order matters: an email's local part can hold digits, and a phone in 966 form must not be half-masked by the identity pattern.
